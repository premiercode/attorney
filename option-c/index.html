Practice areas

Two kinds of work, both about family: changes while everyone is living, and planning for when someone can’t manage their affairs or has passed.

Family Law

Divorce, contested and uncontested. Texas allows a no-fault divorce when a marriage has become “insupportable.” Uncontested means both spouses agree on every issue; contested means they don’t.

Conservatorship (custody) and child support. Texas law calls custody “conservatorship” and visitation “possession and access.” The child’s best interest is always the court’s primary consideration.Conservatorship. Joint managing conservatorship means sharing parental rights and duties, not necessarily equal time.

Modifications and enforcement. Orders can be changed when circumstances have materially and substantially changed. When an order isn’t followed, a court can enforce it.

Premarital and marital property agreements. Texas’s terms for prenups and postnups. They must be in writing and signed by both spouses, and they can’t reduce a child’s right to support.

Adoption. Adoption creates a permanent legal parent-child relationship. Stepparent, relative and agency adoptions are common paths, and the court must find the adoption is in the child’s best interest.

Estate Planning & Probate

Wills, trusts and powers of attorney. A statutory durable power of attorney covers financial matters. A medical power of attorney and a directive to physicians cover health-care choices.

Probate and estate administration. A Texas will generally must be offered for probate within four years of death. Many estates use independent administration, with limited court supervision, and some can use a muniment of title or a small estate affidavit.Muniment of title. When an estate has no unpaid debts other than those secured by real estate, a Texas court may admit a will as a muniment of title, which transfers property without appointing an executor.

Guardianship. A court-supervised arrangement for a minor or an adult who can’t manage their own care or finances. Texas requires the court to consider less restrictive alternatives first, such as powers of attorney or supported decision-making.

Common questions

General information, not legal advice.

Reviewed by: [attorney name]

Is Texas a community property state?

Yes. Property either spouse acquires during the marriage is generally community property. Separate property includes what a spouse owned before marriage and gifts or inheritances received during it. A court divides community property in a way it finds “just and right,” which is not always 50/50.Source. Texas Family Code, sections 3.001 to 3.003 and 7.001.

How long does a Texas divorce take?

A court generally can’t grant a divorce until at least 60 days after the petition is filed, with limited exceptions involving family violence. Many cases take longer.Source. Texas Family Code, section 6.702.

What’s the difference between custody and conservatorship?

None in everyday meaning. Texas law uses “conservatorship.” Joint managing conservatorship means sharing parental rights and duties, not necessarily equal time.Source. Texas Family Code, sections 101.016 and 153.131.

How is child support calculated?

Texas uses guideline percentages of the paying parent’s net resources, starting at 20% for one child, up to a cap that is adjusted over time. As of Sept. 1, 2025, the cap is $11,700 a month in net resources.Source. Texas Family Code, section 154.125.

Does a will avoid probate?

No. A Texas will generally must be admitted to probate to transfer property, though assets with named beneficiaries or survivorship rights can pass outside probate.Source. Texas Estates Code, section 256.001.

Can a financial power of attorney make medical decisions?

No. The statutory durable power of attorney covers property and finances. Health-care decisions need a medical power of attorney.Source. Texas Estates Code, chapter 752; Texas Health and Safety Code, chapter 166.
