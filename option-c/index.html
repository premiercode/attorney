§ 1

Practice areas

Two kinds of work, both about family: the changes that happen while everyone is living, and the planning and paperwork that protect people when someone can’t manage their affairs or has passed.

1.1 Family Law

  1. (a)

    Divorce, contested and uncontested

    Texas allows a no-fault divorce when a marriage has become “insupportable.” Uncontested means both spouses agree on every issue; contested means they don’t.

  2. (b)

    Conservatorship (custody) and child support

    Texas law calls custody “conservatorship” and visitation “possession and access.”2 The child’s best interest is always the court’s primary consideration.

  3. (c)

    Modifications and enforcement

    Orders can be changed when circumstances have materially and substantially changed. When an order isn’t followed, a court can enforce it.

  4. (d)

    Premarital and marital property agreements

    Texas’s terms for prenups and postnups. They must be in writing and signed by both spouses, and they can’t reduce a child’s right to support.

  5. (e)

    Adoption

    Adoption creates a permanent legal parent-child relationship. Stepparent, relative and agency adoptions are common paths, and the court must find the adoption is in the child’s best interest.

1.2 Estate Planning & Probate

  1. (a)

    Wills, trusts and powers of attorney

    A statutory durable power of attorney covers financial matters. A medical power of attorney and a directive to physicians cover health-care choices.

  2. (b)

    Probate and estate administration

    A Texas will generally must be offered for probate within four years of death. Many estates use independent administration, with limited court supervision, and some can use a muniment of title or a small estate affidavit.

  3. (c)

    Guardianship

    A court-supervised arrangement for a minor or an adult who can’t manage their own care or finances. Texas requires the court to consider less restrictive alternatives first, such as powers of attorney or supported decision-making.

2.5 What to bring

Before your first meeting, it helps to gather a photo ID, any court papers already filed, and any existing orders or agreements. For estate planning, bring a list of what you own and how it’s titled, your current beneficiary designations, and any wills or trusts you already have.

No attorney can guarantee the outcome of your case. What we can offer is a clear explanation of the process and your options.

§ 5

Common questions

General information, not legal advice. Reviewed by: [attorney name]

5.1Is Texas a community property state?

Yes. Property either spouse acquires during the marriage is generally community property. Separate property includes what a spouse owned before marriage and gifts or inheritances received during it. A court divides community property in a way it finds “just and right,” which is not always 50/50.8

8 Tex. Fam. Code §§ 3.001–3.003, 7.001.

5.2How long does a Texas divorce take?

A court generally can’t grant a divorce until at least 60 days after the petition is filed, with limited exceptions involving family violence. Many cases take longer.9

9 Tex. Fam. Code § 6.702.

5.3What’s the difference between custody and conservatorship?

None in everyday meaning. Texas law uses “conservatorship.” Joint managing conservatorship means sharing parental rights and duties, not necessarily equal time.10

10 Tex. Fam. Code §§ 101.016, 153.131.

5.4How is child support calculated?

Texas uses guideline percentages of the paying parent’s net resources, starting at 20% for one child, up to a cap that is adjusted over time. As of Sept. 1, 2025, the cap is $11,700 a month in net resources.11

11 Tex. Fam. Code § 154.125.

5.5Does a will avoid probate?

No. A Texas will generally must be admitted to probate to transfer property, though assets with named beneficiaries or survivorship rights can pass outside probate.12

12 Tex. Est. Code § 256.001.

5.6Can a financial power of attorney make medical decisions?

No. The statutory durable power of attorney covers property and finances. Health-care decisions need a medical power of attorney.13

13 Tex. Est. Code ch. 752; Tex. Health & Safety Code ch. 166.

§ 6

Insights

Short, plain-language explainers on the terms that show up in Texas court papers and estate documents.

6.1 Sample article

Why Texas says “conservatorship,” not “custody”

Texas law uses “conservatorship” for a parent’s rights and duties and “possession and access” for parenting time, and knowing the terms makes court papers easier to read.

6.2 Sample article

Muniment of title: a simpler probate path some Texas families can use

When an estate has no unpaid debts other than those secured by real estate, a Texas court may admit a will as a muniment of title, which transfers property without appointing an executor.

6.3 Sample article

Medical power of attorney vs. directive to physicians

A medical power of attorney names someone to make health-care decisions if a doctor certifies you can’t, while a directive to physicians records your own wishes about life-sustaining treatment.
